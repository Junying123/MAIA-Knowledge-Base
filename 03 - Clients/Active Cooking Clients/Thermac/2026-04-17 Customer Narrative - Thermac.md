# Customer Narrative - Thermac Engineering

Thermac Engineering Sdn Bhd is not looking for a cosmetic digitisation of its existing paperwork. The business has grown into a regional equipment and service operation, but much of the day-to-day operating knowledge still sits in Excel, WhatsApp, email, Monday.com calendars, AutoCount, Esoft, physical work order forms, and the memory of experienced staff. MAIA's role is to turn that fragmented operating environment into a structured system that supports sales, service, finance, operations, and management without removing the judgement Thermac's team still needs.

The strongest narrative for Thermac is simple: MAIA gives the company one operating backbone for product sales and service work, while keeping the commercial flexibility that Thermac needs when quoting technical, customer-specific jobs.

## Business Context

Thermac Engineering Sdn Bhd is a specialist mechanical equipment supplier headquartered in Klang, Selangor, with offices in Singapore and Thailand and a partner network extending to Vietnam, Indonesia, and South Korea. The company began in 2014 with a small team and has grown into a regional force in heat transfer technology and fluid handling.

Thermac operates across two tightly connected business arms.

The product business covers heat exchangers, pumps, spare parts, and related equipment. Thermac is the exclusive Southeast Asian representative for ARES, a European plate heat exchanger manufacturer. The product portfolio includes plate, welded, brazed, and finned tube heat exchangers, alongside pumps such as progressive cavity, metering, air-operated double diaphragm, submersible, gear, and lobe pumps under the FLOMAC brand.

The service business covers on-site maintenance, repairs, chemical cleaning, UV inspection, hydro-testing, regasketing, pump refurbishment, spare parts supply, retrofitting, and related field service work. This service arm is strategically important because product sales and service work feed each other. A product customer can become a service customer. A service customer can become a parts or replacement equipment customer. The service history of one equipment unit can become the trigger for future recurring revenue.

That is why MAIA for Thermac cannot be treated as only a quotation-to-invoice setup. It needs to support product sales, service operations, historical pricing, service memory, scheduling visibility, finance visibility, and role-based control.

## Current Operating Picture

Thermac currently manages work through a mix of tools and informal coordination.

| Area | Current Tooling / Method |
|---|---|
| Customer communication | WhatsApp and email |
| Quotation preparation | Excel templates with formula-based costing |
| Accounting and financial records | AutoCount |
| Physical inventory control | Esoft |
| Service scheduling | Monday.com calendars, WhatsApp, phone calls, coordinator memory |
| Service execution | Physical work order forms, photos, signed worksheets, manual updates |
| Customer and service history | Informal records, staff memory, scattered documents |

This setup works because Thermac's people know the business well. Salespeople know how to price unusual requests. Coordinators know which technician can handle which job. Storekeepers know where stock is. Finance understands what needs to flow into AutoCount. But the system does not carry enough of that knowledge. Too much depends on people searching, remembering, checking old files, or stitching information together manually.

## Before MAIA: How Thermac Operates Today

### The Quotation Is A Working Calculator, Not Just A Document

Thermac's quotation process is one of the most important areas to understand. Today, quotations are created using Excel. The Excel file is not only a customer-facing quote layout; it is also the working calculator sales users rely on to decide what amount should be quoted.

The workbook has two key sheets.

| Sheet | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Sheet 1 - Quotation layout | The customer-facing quotation format that is sent to the customer |
| Sheet 2 - Calculation table | A formula-driven working table where users input technical and commercial values to calculate the quoted amount |

The second sheet carries Thermac's pricing logic. Users enter component prices, quantities, and commercial assumptions, then the formulas calculate the amount that flows into the quotation. Example fields include:

- plate price
- gasket price
- frame price
- connection price
- frame parts
- number of plates
- number of gaskets
- margin
- discount

This means Thermac's quotation process is not a simple SKU selection flow. A salesperson may need to quote a standard item, configure plate and gasket quantities, apply component prices, adjust margin, apply discount, consider freight, account for clearance, include transportation cost, handle forex impact, or add a fee such as packaging or delivery.

The current Excel workflow gives the team flexibility, but it also keeps pricing logic outside the system. The final quotation may look clean, but the reasoning behind the price lives in a workbook and in the salesperson's judgement.

### The Quoting Time Sink

Every Thermac quotation is case-by-case. Different clients, equipment types, service scopes, quantities, and commercial assumptions affect the final price. Sales staff have pricing authority, which keeps the process responsive, but the lack of structured pricing memory creates a recurring problem.

When Thermac re-quotes a returning client, the new price has to be defensible against what was previously quoted. If the price changes too much, the customer questions it. Today, the salesperson has to manually search old quotations to find the last quoted price for that customer or item. There is no single searchable pricing history at the point of quote creation.

This creates wasted time and pricing risk. The salesperson either spends time doing document archaeology or proceeds with incomplete context. For service quotations, where pricing can depend on plate counts, gasket counts, margin, discount, and customer-specific history, the problem becomes even more painful.

### Scheduling Is Still A Black Box

Service jobs are coordinated through WhatsApp, phone calls, individual Monday.com calendars, and coordinator memory. There is no unified view of technician assignments, job dates, capacity, conflicts, overdue jobs, or actual job duration.

When a client reschedules, a technician becomes unavailable, or a job takes longer than expected, the coordinator has to mentally reconstruct the schedule. Management only sees conflicts when they surface, rather than seeing them before they become a problem.

This is risky because Thermac's service work often depends on customer downtime windows. If a service slot is missed, the customer's equipment may not be available again soon. If the job still has to be completed in the original window, Thermac may need to reshuffle technicians or absorb overtime cost.

### The Service Job Lifecycle Is Invisible

When a service job is sold, the handoff from sales to operations is manual. A work order is prepared, technicians are assigned, job scope is communicated, worksheets are filled, photos may be sent through WhatsApp, the customer signs off, and invoicing follows.

The issue is that the full lifecycle is not captured in one structured system. The work order form is physical. Notes are scattered. Photos and signed worksheets live in chats or files. Completion status can depend on someone updating a group or telling another person.

This means Thermac lacks a reliable service memory. If a customer asks what was done on a previous job, the answer may depend on finding the technician or searching old messages. If a coordinator changes role, the new person starts with limited context. If management wants to understand job throughput, technician utilisation, delays, or service quality, there is no clean dataset to query.

### Planned Parts And Actual Parts Are Not Cleanly Tracked

Service jobs may require parts to be planned before the job and adjusted after the technician sees the actual site condition. Technicians may bring standby parts, but inventory updates can be missed or delayed. The difference between parts planned and parts actually used is not consistently recorded against the job.

This creates stock drift and weakens service costing. Thermac may know what was expected to be used, but not always what was actually consumed on site.

### Purchase Orders Are Re-Keyed Manually

Customer purchase orders arrive by email. Line items, quantities, descriptions, and prices are manually entered into the next system step. Every re-keying step introduces delay and error risk. Wrong quantities, misread part numbers, or missed pricing details can create downstream correction work.

For a company handling fewer than 100 confirmed sales orders per month, this is not an impossible volume, but it is still a recurring waste of staff time.

### Customer And Equipment Records Are Scattered

Thermac wants to maintain proper records of customer equipment, service history, historical issues, technician notes, parts used, and maintenance timing. Today, this does not exist as a clean system record.

Context is scattered across people, documents, WhatsApp, email, physical forms, and informal memory. A different salesperson or technician may not inherit the full customer story. When staff leave, knowledge leaves with them. There is also no reliable automated reminder system for periodic service follow-up.

### Exchange Rate And Margin Context Is Hard To Carry

Thermac deals with imported products and technical equipment where forex movement can affect margin. Discount decisions may depend on cost assumptions, component pricing, historical customer pricing, and commercial judgement. Today, much of that thinking happens inside Excel or in the salesperson's head.

The pain is not only calculating margin once. It is keeping margin logic, discount logic, and historical price context visible enough for the next person and the next quote.

### Quotation Loss Tracking Is Informal

Lost quotations are not consistently captured in a structured way. Sales staff may know why a deal was lost, but the business does not have clean reporting on loss reasons, pricing objections, follow-up gaps, or competitor pressure.

Without structured loss data, management cannot easily analyse patterns or improve commercial strategy.

### Esoft And AutoCount Create A Duplication Tax

Inventory data is maintained in Esoft while accounting and financial records sit in AutoCount. Storekeepers may be restricted from AutoCount to protect financial data, which is understandable, but it also forces parallel work and creates risk of mismatch.

Different roles need different visibility. Storekeepers need stock context, but not finance data. Sales needs quotations and customer context, but not necessarily all financial controls. Technicians need job context, but not pricing. Finance needs invoice and outstanding account visibility. Management needs oversight across all of it.

Today, the system landscape fragments those responsibilities instead of governing them cleanly.

## After MAIA: What Changes

MAIA should give Thermac a single operating layer across product sales and service operations.

A sales user preparing a quotation should be able to work inside a structured quotation workspace that preserves the useful parts of the current Excel process. The system should support calculation inputs such as plate price, gasket price, frame price, connection price, frame parts, number of plates, number of gaskets, margin, and discount. The user should also see historical pricing references, such as the last five transactions, lifetime average price, and moving average, before sending the quote out.

A coordinator should be able to open a shared calendar or Gantt view and see all active service work orders, technician assignments, dates, conflicts, and workload patterns.

A technician should be able to update a work order with actual work performed, deviations, actual parts used, photos, worksheets, and customer sign-off evidence.

Finance should see invoices, outstanding balances, overdue accounts, and Statement of Account visibility without manually reconstructing the debtor picture.

Management should see quotation loss trends, work order progress, service activity, overdue accounts, and operational visibility without waiting for someone to compile a report.

Every user should see only what they need. MAIA should reduce duplicate entry and fragmented access by giving each role the right workspace, the right permissions, and the right business context.

## Feature Narrative

### 1. Flexible Quotation And Pricing Intelligence

This is the heart of the sales-side story. MAIA needs to support Thermac's quotation process as a working commercial calculator, not only a final document generator.

The current Excel structure should guide the new workflow:

- a customer-facing quotation output
- a calculation layer for technical and commercial inputs
- support for plate price, gasket price, frame price, connection price, frame parts, number of plates, and number of gaskets
- support for margin and discount calculation
- support for commercial factors such as freight, clearance, transportation fee, and forex where required
- visibility of historical pricing while the salesperson is quoting

Pricing intelligence should surface useful historical references at the point of quote creation:

- last five transactions for the relevant customer-item pair
- lifetime average price
- moving average, such as a 90-day moving average where available
- previous or latest price
- minimum and maximum price boundaries where available
- standard price or customer-specific price where available

This addresses the quoting time sink by reducing the need to open old quotations manually. It also gives sales a point of reference before the customer challenges a price.

### 2. PO-To-Sales Order Conversion

When a customer sends a purchase order by email, MAIA should parse the document through the CPO or intelligent document processing flow, extract line items, quantities, descriptions, pricing, and relevant order data, then create a draft review record.

The user reviews and corrects the extracted information before confirming. MAIA then creates the sales order from the reviewed data.

This addresses the manual purchase order bottleneck by shifting user effort from re-keying to validation. MAIA should not auto-confirm orders; the human stays in control.

### 3. Product Sales Document Lifecycle

Thermac still needs the standard sales backbone:

- quotation
- sales order
- invoice
- delivery note
- customer-signed delivery note
- credit note handling within supported MAIA behaviour

This addresses the need for a cleaner product-sales operating foundation while preserving AutoCount as the accounting reference where required.

### 4. Service Work Order Management

When a service job is sold, MAIA should create a structured work order linked to the customer, equipment, and sales context.

The work order should capture:

- job scope
- customer and equipment details
- technician assignment
- scheduled dates
- job type
- planned parts
- actual parts used
- actual work performed
- deviations from the planned work
- photos
- worksheets
- customer sign-off evidence
- audit trail
- 2-page work order PDF output

This addresses the invisible job lifecycle by making the service job traceable from sale to completion. It also addresses parts tracking by separating planned parts from actual parts used.

### 5. Calendar And Gantt Scheduling Views

MAIA should provide calendar and Gantt scheduling views for service work orders. Coordinators and managers should be able to see technician workload, assigned jobs, job dates, conflicts, and schedule movement.

This addresses the scheduling black box by moving planning out of individual calendars and memory. It does not need to auto-schedule jobs. The planner remains the decision-maker, but with better visibility.

### 6. Customer Service History And Equipment Records

MAIA should create a service memory layer around customers and equipment. Completed work orders, technician notes, job photos, worksheets, parts used, service dates, and follow-up context should become part of the customer and equipment history.

This addresses the scattered customer and equipment record problem. When a customer asks what was done previously, Thermac should be able to answer from MAIA rather than searching chats or asking a technician to remember.

### 7. Proactive Service Reminders

Maintenance-type work orders should include a next maintenance date or service follow-up date. MAIA should trigger reminders to the relevant salesperson, service coordinator, or role when follow-up is due.

This addresses the lack of reliable service follow-up. It turns completed service work into future pipeline and helps Thermac manage recurring relationships more systematically.

### 8. Statement Of Account Visibility

MAIA should provide Statement of Account visibility for finance and, where agreed, customer-facing access through a secure link or controlled view.

Finance users should be able to see open invoices, overdue invoices, outstanding customer balances, and overdue flags based on configured payment terms or grace periods.

This addresses the manual effort of monitoring outstanding accounts. It does not automate collections; finance still drives follow-up.

### 9. CRM Interaction Logging

MAIA should support lightweight CRM interaction logging: calls, visit notes, follow-up reminders, account updates, and service context. This gives authorised users a shared view of customer relationship history.

This addresses the problem of relationship context living in people's heads. It also supports better quoting, service follow-up, and management visibility.

### 10. Quotation Loss Tracking

MAIA should allow quotations to be marked as lost with a structured reason and free-text explanation. Lost quotation data should feed a reporting view for management.

This addresses the informal loss-tracking problem. Instead of relying on memory, Thermac can analyse recurring reasons for lost deals and refine pricing, follow-up, and sales strategy.

### 11. Role-Based Access And Workspaces

MAIA should organise access by role and workspace.

| Workspace / Role | What It Should Support |
|---|---|
| Sales Agent Workspace | Quotation management, sales order management, customer management, output documents, pricing reference view, lost quotation reporting |
| Finance Workspace | Invoice visibility, Statement of Account, overdue flags, finance follow-up support |
| Operations and Storekeeper Workspace | Inventory reference access, delivery support, role-limited operational updates |
| Technician Access | Assigned work orders, job updates, actual work performed, parts used, attachments |
| Management Workspace | Operational visibility, quotation loss reporting, outstanding accounts, role-based oversight |

This addresses the duplication and access-control problem. Storekeepers should not need full AutoCount finance access to update operational stock context. Technicians should not need pricing visibility to update job progress. Sales should see what they need for customer and quotation work. Finance and management need broader visibility without exposing everything to everyone.

### 12. AutoCount And Esoft Integration Context

MAIA should reduce duplicate entry and improve data consistency across the current system landscape. AutoCount remains important for accounting and financial records. Esoft remains relevant to physical inventory context unless replaced or integrated differently.

The intended integration direction should support:

- reading master data such as customers, items, pricing, credit terms, and inventory where available
- writing transactions such as sales orders, invoices, delivery notes, and credit notes where approved
- using API integration where possible, with file-based formats such as CSV, Excel, or XML as fallback where needed

This addresses the duplication tax by reducing repeated entry across systems and giving each role a cleaner way to participate without exposing unnecessary data.

## Scope Summary

The current RM35,000 implementation narrative should be understood as covering a combined product-sales and service-operations foundation:

- flexible quotation and pricing intelligence
- PO-to-sales order conversion
- standard sales document lifecycle
- AutoCount integration context
- role-based access and workspaces
- service work order management
- calendar and Gantt scheduling views
- customer service history and equipment records
- proactive service reminders
- Statement of Account visibility
- CRM interaction logging
- quotation loss tracking

Items such as AI-driven scheduling optimisation, fully automated collections, automatic order confirmation, and fully predictive maintenance should remain outside the current narrative unless separately scoped.

## Design Principle

MAIA should not replace Thermac's team. It should structure the environment they operate in.

The sales process needs flexibility because Thermac quotes technical, case-by-case work. The service process needs structure because field jobs, parts usage, scheduling, and customer history cannot keep living in scattered tools. Finance needs visibility without manual reconstruction. Operations needs stock and delivery context without unnecessary financial exposure. Management needs a reliable view of what is happening across the business.

The principle for Thermac is:

> Keep the front of the process flexible, make the confirmed work structured, and keep the downstream record auditable.

That is what MAIA should provide: a shared operating system that helps Thermac quote better, schedule better, service better, follow up better, and manage the business with less dependence on memory.
