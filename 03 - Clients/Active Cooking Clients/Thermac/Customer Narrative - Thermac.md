## Who Thermac Is

Thermac Engineering Sdn Bhd is a specialist mechanical equipment supplier headquartered in Klang, Selangor, with offices in Singapore and Thailand and a partner network extending to Vietnam, Indonesia, and South Korea. Founded in 2014 with two to three people, the company has grown into a regional force in heat transfer technology and fluid handling, having supplied over 300 units of equipment across Southeast Asia to date.

The business operates across two tightly coupled arms.

**The product business** — Thermac is the exclusive Southeast Asian representative for ARES, a European plate heat exchanger manufacturer. They carry a broad portfolio of heat exchangers — plate, welded, brazed, finned tube — alongside a pumps division covering progressive cavity, metering, air-operated double diaphragm, submersible, gear, and lobe pumps under their registered FLOMAC brand. They serve industries spanning HVAC, district cooling, oil and gas, pharmaceutical, pulp and paper, palm oil, food and beverage, glove manufacturing, and power generation. Their equipment has been delivered to Petronas offshore platforms, Samsung-Shell projects, Keppel Sakra's 600MW combined cycle plant, Sunway Medical Centre, and major district cooling plants across KL.

**The service business** — and this is where the MAIA build matters most. Thermac provides on-site maintenance, repair, chemical cleaning, UV inspection, hydro-testing, regasketing, pump refurbishment, and spare parts supply and retrofitting for heat exchangers and pumps of all brands, not just their own. They hold servicing contracts with major industrial clients. Their service team doubled in 2020 and has been the backbone of significant contracts — including maintaining over 150 plate heat exchangers for a major glove manufacturer and servicing Petronas offshore platforms.

These two arms feed each other. A customer who buys a plate heat exchanger from Thermac becomes a service customer. A customer whose equipment was supplied by a competitor still comes to Thermac for servicing and spare parts. The product business generates the installed base; the service business generates the recurring relationships and revenue.

The product side of the business — sales orders, quotations, invoicing, delivery — can run on MAIA's existing out-of-the-box modules with standard configuration. Thermac handles below 100 confirmed sales orders monthly across a team of four salespeople. Orders come in via WhatsApp and email. POs are received, sales orders are created, goods are delivered, invoices are issued. This is the standard document lifecycle that MAIA already supports.

The service side is where Thermac needs custom capability. The complexity of scheduling technicians, tracking service jobs from sale to completion, maintaining equipment and customer history, and building institutional memory around what was done, when, where, and by whom — none of that exists in a structured system today. That is what this project builds.

## Before MAIA: How Thermac Operates Today

Thermac's service business runs on people, relationships, and experience. That's a strength. But the systems behind those people are fragmented, manual, and invisible to anyone who isn't directly involved in a given job.

### The Scheduling Black Box

Service jobs — maintenance visits, emergency repairs, cleaning contracts, new installations — are coordinated through a combination of WhatsApp messages, individual Monday.com calendars, phone calls, and a coordinator's memory. There is no unified view of who is assigned where, when jobs are due, or how long they actually take. Each salesperson and coordinator maintains their own calendar. There is no shared view of team capacity, ETAs, or job dates.

When a client reschedules, when a technician falls sick, or when a job runs longer than expected, the coordinator must mentally reconstruct the entire schedule to figure out what shifts. With a limited technician pool and jobs varying wildly in size — a quick gasket replacement is half a day; a full PHE overhaul at an offshore platform is a multi-day affair — this isn't coordination. It's juggling blind.

There is no record of the delta between when a job was created, when it was scheduled, and when it was completed. No way to measure whether a two-day job actually took two days or five. No way to see, at a glance, that three jobs are stacked on the same week while the following week sits empty. The planning function exists entirely in one person's head, and manager approval is only triggered when scheduling conflicts have already happened rather than being visible before they occur.

### The Quoting Time Sink

Every deal Thermac quotes is case-by-case. Different clients, different equipment, different service scopes — each quotation is built from scratch using Excel-based pricing templates with formulas. Sales staff have full pricing authority without finance involvement, which keeps things fast but introduces a specific problem.

The pain is not lost margin. The pain is that when Thermac re-quotes a returning client, the new price has to be defensible against what was previously quoted. If the price swings too far in either direction — up or down — the client flags it as an exception. That creates friction, delays approvals, and erodes trust.

Today, defending that price means the salesperson has to manually open past quotations one by one to find what was quoted last time for that specific customer-item pair. There is no searchable history. No centralised pricing lookup. That's time burned on archaeology instead of selling. And when the historical price can't be found quickly, the user either guesses or quotes conservatively — both of which carry cost. Multiply this across roughly 7 to 8 service inquiries per month on top of the product quotations, and the cumulative time waste is real.

Service pricing carries an additional layer: a separate pricing template calculates service costs based on unit counts like plates, forming a minimum price floor. Sales staff then adjust margins per customer. Each customer effectively has a distinct pricing profile for service work. Without historical pricing visibility, maintaining pricing consistency across these profiles is an exercise in memory.

### The Invisible Job Lifecycle

When a service job is sold, the handoff from sales to operations runs through a manual flow: scheduling, technician assignment, a two-page worksheet (page one before the job with quoted details and parts needed, page two after the job with actual work performed and deviations), customer sign-off, then invoicing. None of this is structured in a system. The work order form exists as a physical document. Notes live in WhatsApp threads. Photos and signed worksheets are shared via group chat — not attached to any job record. Completion status lives in someone's WhatsApp reply.

This means Thermac has no institutional memory of its service operations. When a client calls and asks "what was done to our chiller last March?", the answer requires phoning the technician who was there and hoping they remember. When a new coordinator takes over, they start from zero. When management wants to understand utilisation, job throughput, or service quality, there is no data to query.

Stock tracking compounds the problem. Technicians take standby parts to site, but inventory updates are sometimes missed. What was planned versus what was actually used is not formally recorded against the job, causing quantity drift between what's in the warehouse and what the system says.

### The Purchase Order Bottleneck

When a customer sends a purchase order, the details — line items, quantities, descriptions, pricing — are manually re-keyed into the system. This is slow, error-prone, and unnecessary. Every manual transcription step is a point where a wrong quantity, a misread part number, or a transposed digit can slip through. For a team handling up to 100 sales orders monthly, this is a compounding inefficiency.

### Customer and Equipment Records — Scattered

Thermac wants to maintain CRM records of each customer's equipment units and service history. Today, that doesn't exist. There is no structured per-unit service record. Context is lost when a different salesperson or technician handles the same customer. There are no automated reminders for periodic service. Forecasting service needs relies on memory. When a staff member leaves, the knowledge leaves with them.

Exchange rate fluctuations impact margin calculations for imported products, complicating discount decisions. Quotation loss tracking is informal — sales staff rely on memory rather than system analysis for why deals were lost.

### The Duplication Tax

Inventory data is entered separately into ESoft for inventory control and AutoCount for accounting, creating double work. The storekeeper is restricted from AutoCount access to protect financial data integrity, but this forces parallel systems and creates risk of stock discrepancies. Different roles need access to different parts of the process, but the current setup fragments rather than governs that access.

## After MAIA: What Changes

MAIA gives Thermac a structured operating system — one platform for both the product business and the service business. The product side leverages MAIA's out-of-the-box modules for the standard sales document lifecycle: quotations, sales orders, delivery notes, invoices, credit notes. The service side gets the custom capability that doesn't exist today.

Here is what Thermac's operation looks like with MAIA in place:

**A coordinator opens MAIA in the morning** and sees every active work order on a shared calendar. She can see which technicians are assigned to which jobs this week, which jobs are overdue, which are scheduled but not started, and where there's capacity. When a client calls to reschedule, she can see the knock-on impact on other jobs immediately. No more checking individual Monday.com calendars. No WhatsApp. No guessing.

**A sales user prepares a re-quote for a returning client.** Before typing a single number, MAIA surfaces the last five transactions, the lifetime average price, and the 90-day moving average for that specific customer-item pair — right there on the quotation screen. The user doesn't need to open past quotations one by one. They see the pricing context, make the call, and move on. If the new price deviates significantly from history, they know it before the client does.

**A customer sends a purchase order by email.** Instead of someone retyping it line by line, MAIA parses the PO through the CPO module, extracts the structured data, and presents a draft sales order for review. The team validates and confirms. The data entry grunt work — and the errors that come with it — are eliminated.

**A service job is created from a sales order.** A formal work order is issued in MAIA — capturing the customer, the equipment, the scope, the assigned technician, the scheduled date, and the parts planned for the job. The technician goes on-site, completes the work, records what was actually done (including any deviations from the plan), logs the parts actually used, uploads photos and signed worksheets directly to the work order, and the customer signs off. The completed work order generates a clean PDF, updates the customer's service timeline, feeds into the equipment's maintenance history, and captures the next expected maintenance date.

**Six months later, the maintenance cycle is due.** MAIA sends a notification to the assigned salesperson because the next maintenance date was captured at completion. The sales user opens the customer record and sees the full service history — every job, every note, every interaction — without phoning anyone. They reach out proactively. That's not just follow-up; that's client retention and recurring revenue generated by the system itself.

**Management wants to review operations.** They can see total active work orders, time from creation to scheduling to completion, technician workload patterns, and a full service timeline per customer — all without asking anyone to compile a report. Lost quotation trends are visible. Outstanding accounts are flagged. The finance team has a debtor Statement of Account view that can even be shared with customers via secure link.

**Every user sees only what they need.** The storekeeper updates inventory without seeing financial data. The technician updates work order progress without accessing pricing. Sales handles quotations and customer follow-up. Finance monitors invoices and overdue accounts. Operations coordinates everything. Management has full visibility. One system, role-based boundaries, no duplication.

## Feature Deep Dive

### 1. Work Order Management — The Core Build

This is the centrepiece of the MAIA build for Thermac, and where the majority of the RM35,000 investment is concentrated. It directly addresses the biggest operational gap: the absence of any formal system for tracking service jobs from sale to completion.

**What it does:** When a service job is sold, a Work Order is created from the Sales Order. That Work Order captures the customer and equipment details, the quoted job scope and parts required (page one of Thermac's current worksheet), the technician assigned, the scheduled dates, the job type (installation, repair, maintenance, cleaning), and the status. As the job progresses, the technician records actual work performed, deviations from the plan, and parts actually used (page two). Photos and signed worksheets are attached directly to the work order — no more WhatsApp group threads as the filing system. It generates a professional 2-page PDF — for internal use or to hand to the client on-site. It carries a full audit trail: who created it, who updated it, and when.

**Maintenance-type Work Orders:** For recurring maintenance jobs, the Work Order includes a "Next Maintenance Date" field. This field serves as a data point and trigger for a custom notification that pings the relevant users when the next maintenance cycle is approaching. This turns Thermac's service history from a passive record into an active tool for client retention and recurring revenue.

**Parts tracking:** The Work Order supports listing parts planned for the job and recording parts actually used during the job. This gives the office visibility into service-related stock usage and reduces the inventory drift that comes from untracked on-site consumption.

**What it won't do:** Job progression is not automated. MAIA will not move a Work Order from "In Progress" to "Completed" on its own — a person updates the status. This is deliberate. In field service, reality is messy. Machines surprise you. Schedules shift. Forcing automation onto job status would create more problems than it solves. The system provides the structure; the team provides the discipline.

### 2. Calendar and Gantt Scheduling Views

This is the visibility layer that Thermac's planning function currently lacks entirely.

Thermac's technician pool is limited. Jobs vary in size. Clients reschedule. Emergency calls come in. Today, scheduling lives in individual Monday.com calendars with no unified view of team capacity, ETAs, and job dates. Without a shared visual of the schedule, the coordinator carries the entire picture in their head, and everyone else is working blind.

**What it does:** MAIA provides Calendar and Gantt-based views of all active Work Orders. Planners see technician workload, job timelines, and scheduling conflicts at a glance. Critically, the system captures timestamps at each stage — creation, scheduling, and completion — which means Thermac can start measuring the deltas: how long from sale to scheduled start? How long from start to completion? Where are the bottlenecks? Updates to work order dates or status are reflected in the calendar in real time.

**What it won't do:** There is no auto-scheduling or algorithmic optimisation. The planner remains the decision-maker. Manager approval continues to be needed only when scheduling conflicts arise. MAIA makes sure the planner is making that decision with full visibility instead of working from memory.

### 3. Pricing Intelligence

Thermac's quoting process is case-by-case by nature. Different clients, scopes, equipment types, and service conditions mean every quotation is bespoke — built from Excel-based pricing templates with separate cost calculation templates for service jobs. The pain is not inconsistent pricing per se — it's the time cost of finding the right price and the client-facing risk of getting it wrong.

**What it does:** MAIA displays historical pricing intelligence for every customer-item pair during quotation, sales order, and sales invoice creation:

- **Last 5 transactions** for that specific customer-item pair (sourced from past quotations, sales orders, and invoices)
- **Lifetime average price** for the item across all customers
- **90-day moving average** for the item

This is advisory, not enforcement — MAIA will not block a price. But it eliminates the need for the salesperson to manually open past quotations one by one. The context is surfaced at the point of decision, instantly.

**Why it matters:** When a returning client sees a price that's significantly different from last time, they flag it as an exception. That triggers back-and-forth, delays approvals, and erodes confidence. By surfacing historical pricing at the point of entry, MAIA lets the sales user calibrate before the quote goes out — not after the client pushes back. For service quotations where each customer has a distinct pricing profile, this is especially critical.

### 4. PO-to-Sales Order Conversion

**What it does:** When a customer purchase order comes in, MAIA's CPO module parses the PO document, extracts the structured data — line items, quantities, descriptions, pricing — and creates a CPO record. The user reviews and manually edits the extracted information where needed, then confirms. MAIA creates the sales order from the CPO record and the user proceeds with the normal downstream order process.

**What it won't do:** MAIA will not auto-confirm any order. This is human-in-the-loop by design. The value is shifting the team's effort from data entry (where mistakes creep in) to validation (where their judgment matters).

### 5. Customer Service History and Equipment Records

This is where Thermac starts building institutional memory that stays with the business, not with individual staff members.

**What it does:** Every Work Order, every interaction note, every logged activity against a customer is aggregated into a service timeline. Equipment records are created within the customer page — when a customer buys new equipment, it gets added to the record. When a work order is created, it's linked to the relevant equipment. The completed work order becomes part of that equipment's service history, including the technician's comments on work performed.

When a technician walks into a client's site, the team can pull up everything that's been done there — what was serviced, when, by whom, and what was noted. This is lightweight CRM — not a full Salesforce replacement, but enough to stop knowledge from walking out the door when a staff member leaves or when a different salesperson handles the same account.

**The constraint:** It depends on consistent logging. If the team doesn't log interactions, the timeline stays empty. MAIA provides the container; the team fills it.

**Within this scope:** The architecture supports customer-level service history with equipment records linked to work orders. The groundwork here — work orders linked to customers and equipment, service timelines, audit trails, maintenance date triggers — makes future extensions (per-unit lifecycle tracking, warranty status, service interval analytics) straightforward.

### 6. Proactive Service Reminders

**What it does:** Each equipment record stores the latest completed service date. MAIA calculates or displays the next expected service timing based on the service interval. When the reminder threshold is reached, MAIA prompts the assigned salesperson to follow up with the customer about scheduling the next service.

**Why it matters:** Today, proactive follow-up relies on manual memory. No one is systematically tracking which customers are due for their next service. This feature turns completed jobs into future pipeline. It's the seed of proactive maintenance intelligence — and within this scope, it's implemented through the next maintenance date field on maintenance-type work orders with notification triggers.

### 7. Statement of Account

**What it does:** A dedicated Statement of Account view for both internal finance use and customer-facing access. Internally, finance users see a debtor view showing open invoices, overdue invoices, and total outstanding per customer. MAIA flags overdue accounts based on invoice due date plus a configured grace period (e.g., 30-day terms + 10 days = trigger at day 40). The finance team gets notified when follow-up is due.

Externally, MAIA can generate or expose a customer-facing Statement of Account view — accessible through a secure link or QR code — so the customer can check what's outstanding without waiting for manual confirmation.

**What it won't do:** It will not automate collections. The finance team still drives follow-ups — but with a dashboard and triggers instead of manually monitoring aging reports.

### 8. CRM Interaction Logging

Call logs, visit notes, follow-up reminders — this comes out of the box with MAIA. Users can add interaction notes under the customer record: visit notes, call summaries, general account updates. The interaction history is visible to authorised users when reviewing the customer account, supporting future quotation, service, and work order handling. Minimal configuration needed.

### 9. Quotation Loss Tracking

**What it does:** Quotations can be marked as lost with a structured reason (dropdown) and detailed free-text explanation. Lost quotation data feeds into a reporting view so management can identify recurring commercial issues and improve pricing, follow-up, or sales strategy over time.

The recommended approach (Option 2 from the technical requirements) preserves the current quotation-level user experience while syncing the loss outcome to the linked Opportunity record as the backend reporting source. This gives cleaner analysis when multiple quotation revisions exist for one deal, without changing how sales users interact with the system day-to-day.

### 10. Role-Based Access and Permissions

**What it does:** MAIA assigns access based on user role. Each role sees only the modules, records, and actions relevant to their function:

- **Storekeeper:** Updates inventory without access to pricing or financial data — eliminating the need for the parallel ESoft/AutoCount dual-entry system
- **Technician:** Views assigned work orders and updates job progress without seeing pricing
- **Sales:** Accesses customer records, quotations, sales orders, and service context for their accounts
- **Finance:** Monitors invoices, payments, overdue accounts, and SOA without accessing all operational functions
- **Operations/Admin:** Coordinates across inventory, work orders, scheduling, and supporting documents
- **Management:** High-level visibility across functions with role-based boundaries for day-to-day users

This eliminates the current duplication where data is entered separately into ESoft and AutoCount because different roles need different access levels.

### 11. Product Sales — Out of the Box

Thermac's product distribution business — selling heat exchangers, pumps, and related equipment — leverages MAIA's standard modules. Sales orders, quotations, invoicing, delivery notes, credit notes, and inventory management for stocked items all run on MAIA's existing document lifecycle. The pricing intelligence feature applies equally to product quotations. For purchase-on-demand items (not true dropship — Thermac receives the goods then sends them out), the standard procurement-to-delivery flow handles the process.

This is not a custom build. It's the same MAIA backbone that supports other clients, configured for Thermac's product catalog, payment terms (up to 90-day credit for regular customers, down payments for new customers), and document formats. The integration with AutoCount for invoicing compliance is maintained.

### Designed For, Not Included (Phase 2)

- **Individual Asset/Equipment Lifecycle Tracking** — per-unit maintenance history, warranty status, and service intervals for specific machines (e.g., "PHE Unit #3 at Client X's Petaling Jaya plant"). The architecture built in Phase 1 is specifically designed to extend into this.
- **Proactive Maintenance Intelligence** — automated reminders based on past job intervals, predictive scheduling, and "this machine was last serviced 11 months ago" alerts. The maintenance date notification in Phase 1 is the seed; this is the full growth.
- **Advanced Scheduling** — manpower capacity planning, daily resource limits, and flexible capacity management for unplanned absences.
- **Exchange Rate Impact Analysis** — margin calculation support for imported products with currency fluctuation visibility.

### Requires Clarification

- **Purchase-on-demand items** — Does Thermac maintain stock for these? Do they key items into the stock system before delivery? Is serial/batch tracking needed? Partial receipt/delivery? Should these affect on-hand stock or pass through staging?

### Not in Scope

- Automated job progression or workflow automation
- AI-driven scheduling optimisation
- Automated collections or payment follow-ups
- Full CRM with pipeline management
- Auto-confirmation of any orders or transactions

## The Design Principle

MAIA does not replace Thermac's team. It structures the environment they operate in. Every feature in this build follows the same philosophy: MAIA handles the grunt work — parsing documents, surfacing historical data, organising records, generating reports, triggering reminders — while humans stay in the driver's seat for every decision that matters.

For a company that has grown from a 2–3 person operation in 2014 to a regional service force with offices in three countries, major industrial contracts across Southeast Asia, and an installed base of over 300 units of equipment, the gap between Thermac's operational ambition and its operational infrastructure is real. That gap is what creates the firefighting, the missed follow-ups, the scheduling chaos, and the dependence on individual memory over institutional systems.

The product distribution business runs on MAIA's standard modules — that's table stakes. The service business is where the custom value lives. Work order management, shared scheduling, proactive maintenance reminders, equipment history, pricing intelligence — these are the capabilities that turn Thermac from a company that reacts to service requests into one that systematically manages, tracks, and grows its service relationships.

The RM35,000 investment buys Thermac a proper operational backbone for both arms of the business: products and service, orders and jobs, customers and equipment, schedules and financials — all in one system, with the visibility and control that's been missing. It's not everything. But it's the foundation that makes everything else possible.
