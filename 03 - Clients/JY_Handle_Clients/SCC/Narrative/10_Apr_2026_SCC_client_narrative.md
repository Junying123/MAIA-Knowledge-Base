# Understanding SCC Corporation Sdn Bhd: How The Business Operates Today And Where MAIA Can Reduce Friction

This narrative is based on SCC requirements gathering notes, follow-up requirements questions, and SCC's public company positioning. It is written to help the team understand how the business likely operates day to day, where coordination becomes heavy, what SCC's current operating style feels like, and why the proposed MAIA capabilities matter.

## Business Context

SCC Corporation Sdn Bhd operates across two main business divisions: Foodservice Equipment and Animal Health Product. Publicly, the company positions the foodservice side around commercial kitchen and beverage equipment, while the animal health side focuses on feed-related and animal health solutions for feed mills and farms. In the working context captured so far, SCC is also framed around selling cooking machines and animal feed, with target customers that include cinemas, convenience stores, and farms.

That combination matters. SCC is not running one simple, straight-line sales motion. It is serving different buyer types, different product contexts, and different commercial realities that still need to be coordinated through one business. A quotation may begin with sales, but pricing may depend on purchasing input. A stock question may begin with a customer request, but the answer depends on what other salespeople are already holding. Finance may need to judge overdue exposure not only by one salesperson's account, but across the whole customer relationship. This is the kind of operating environment where business truth is spread across teams rather than sitting neatly in one place.

Based on the current notes, MAIA is not being evaluated as a headcount replacement tool. SCC's stated goals are to save employee time, reduce repetitive work, and improve efficiency. That means they will judge MAIA less by surface-level automation and more by whether it reduces coordination burden without disrupting the real way the business runs.

## A Day In The Life Of The Company

At SCC, the day likely starts with incoming requests landing through multiple channels at once. Customers ask for quotations, product availability, delivery timing, or past document references. Sales and admin users are the first to absorb that demand, but they are rarely working from a single source of truth. A quotation request may trigger a pricing check. A stock question may trigger a reservation check. A returning customer may require someone to understand not only what was quoted before, but what is still owed across the wider account.

By mid-morning, the work begins to spread across functions. Sales may need purchasing input before finalizing a quote because pricing is dynamic and may not be cleanly maintained in SAP. Warehouse planning has to think not just about what exists physically, but what is already informally or operationally committed. A simple customer conversation can quickly turn into internal coordination: check with purchasing, confirm with sales support, verify reservation status, then decide whether the business can commit.

By midday and afternoon, exceptions and follow-up work start to accumulate. A salesperson may need to ask why a price differs from last time. Another may need visibility into who is holding stock that looks unavailable. Finance may need to understand whether a customer's overdue exposure should be viewed at department level or whole-company level. Someone may need an older invoice or a chopped signed delivery order that is not maintained in SAP. Management may want a clearer picture of what happened in the field, but the update is still sitting inside a voice note or informal follow-up rather than a structured record.

By the end of the day, SCC likely has most things moving, but not always in a cleanly visible way. Warehouse is preparing for the next day's pick-and-pack activity. Sales has moved quotations forward, but some items may still depend on approval or clarification. Finance may still be stitching together overdue visibility and reconciliation context. Outdoor sales may have generated useful customer intelligence, but that intelligence still needs to be translated into something management can use. The work gets done, but much of it is held together by people carrying context across systems, chats, spreadsheets, and memory.

## Current Company DNA

Based on the requirements context so far, SCC's current operating DNA appears commercially responsive, relationship-aware, and highly manual.

The business does not sound careless or unstructured in intent. It sounds like a company that knows how to keep work moving in the real world, even when process logic is not fully systemized. When something is urgent, people likely solve it by checking with the right person, following up through chat or direct contact, and using experience to bridge the gap between what the system says and what the business actually knows. When information is missing, the answer seems to come more often from a person or spreadsheet than from a clean shared workflow.

This suggests an operating style that is strong in responsiveness but weaker in shared visibility. It is likely fast-moving in commercial handling, but fragile in the places where pricing, reservation ownership, approvals, and historical records need traceability. In short, SCC seems to be a business that runs on human coordination first and system structure second.

## Why Operations Feel Heavy Today

Pricing is one of the clearest sources of weight. SCC's notes describe pricing as dynamic, customer-specific, and often maintained outside SAP. The purchasing manager may give prices directly to sales, and the pricing logic may sit in Excel rather than in a master list that everyone can rely on. That means quoting is not just a sales action. It is a coordination action. Users have to move quickly while also avoiding inconsistency, expired context, or a price that cannot later be defended.

Stock visibility is heavy for a different reason. SCC does not only want to know available quantity. They want to know who is holding reserved stock, under which sales order, for which customer, and under which salesperson. That means the real problem is not inventory display. The real problem is reservation ownership. Without that visibility, users may see a number but still not know whether the stock can actually be negotiated, released, or reallocated.

Finance feels heavy because the business needs a better view across related but fragmented tasks: customer overdue visibility, credit limit approval, statement of account handling, knock-off support, invoice tagging, and daily reporting. These are all signals that finance currently depends on manual interpretation and follow-up to understand what the business should do next.

Field visibility also feels heavy because useful account information is being generated informally. Voice notes after meetings, verbal updates, and ad hoc reporting can all be valuable, but they do not automatically become structured company knowledge. Management may know that activity is happening, but not have a clean way to retrieve what was discussed, promised, or quoted later.

Finally, the go-live concern shows that SCC is not only thinking about future workflow. They are thinking about business continuity. If MAIA starts from SAP sync at go-live, but older invoices and signed delivery records still live in folders outside SAP, then users will continue to experience friction whenever the business needs to look backward as well as forward.

## User Narratives

### Indoor Sales And Sales Support

Before MAIA, an indoor sales or sales support user likely begins the day handling quotation requests, customer clarifications, and document follow-up from several channels at once. The work is not simply "prepare the quote and send it." They may need to check with purchasing for the latest price, verify whether the quotation is still valid, confirm if the customer has been priced differently before, or ensure that stock is not already tied up elsewhere. Even after preparing the document, approval may still sit between the user and SAP posting. The emotional texture here is constant low-grade pressure: move fast, do not quote wrongly, do not miss a control step, and do not make a promise the rest of the business cannot fulfill.

After MAIA, this role should feel less like a coordinator of loose ends. Pricing requests should be easier to trigger and trace. Approval flow should be visible instead of implied. Stock should show both quantity and reservation context. The user still needs judgment, but less energy should go into chasing information and more into responding confidently to the customer.

### Purchasing And Pricing Control

Before MAIA, the pricing owner on the purchasing side likely functions as a living control point for commercial truth. Because pricing appears dynamic and not always reflected immediately in SAP, the team becomes dependent on purchasing to validate or provide the right number at the right moment. This makes purchasing central to speed and accuracy, but it also creates bottlenecks and person-dependency.

After MAIA, the goal is not to remove purchasing from the process. The goal is to structure that interaction so that pricing requests, responses, and history become easier to manage. That should reduce the risk of pricing logic living only in one spreadsheet or one experienced person's hands.

### Warehouse And Delivery Planning

Before MAIA, warehouse planning appears to rely on SAP-generated pick-and-pack information combined with day-end preparation for the next day. The team has to think about what to pack, how much to pack, and how delivery orders might be grouped by region. But planning quality still depends on whether stock availability really reflects operational reality. If reservation rules are situational and ownership is unclear, the warehouse team is planning around uncertainty rather than around trusted visibility.

After MAIA, warehouse and delivery planning should feel more controlled. The team should be able to understand not only what needs to move, but what is already committed and why. Even without automating every logistics decision, MAIA should make daily planning less dependent on hidden context.

### Finance

Before MAIA, finance likely experiences the business as a chain of partial visibility problems. Outstanding exposure may need to be understood across the whole customer relationship, not just one department. Credit limit decisions may depend on information that is not surfaced cleanly. SOA handling and knock-off support likely require manual checking and confirmation. Daily sales reporting may still depend on someone collecting and stitching the picture together.

After MAIA, finance should be able to work with clearer visibility and less manual reconstruction. Overdue amounts should be easier to interpret, daily reporting easier to surface, and reconciliation support more structured. The real benefit is not simply speed. It is confidence in what the numbers mean and what action should happen next.

### Outdoor Sales And Management

Before MAIA, outdoor sales and management likely live with a visibility gap between customer-facing activity and internal knowledge. A salesperson finishes a visit, sends a voice note, and moves on to the next account. Useful commercial context exists, but it remains informal unless someone later compiles it into a report. Management therefore sees only fragments unless follow-up discipline is unusually strong.

After MAIA, the business should retain more of that field intelligence in usable form. The salesperson should still be able to work naturally, but what was discussed, quoted, or promised should be easier to capture and retrieve later. For management, that means seeing the motion of the business more clearly instead of waiting only for downstream outcomes.

## What This Means For MAIA

The strongest implication from this narrative is that MAIA's value for SCC will come from reducing coordination drag and improving trust in operational truth.

Pricing workflow matters because SCC appears to depend on dynamic, customer-specific, and partly off-system pricing logic. Approval workflow matters because documents do not become operationally real until the right authority has signed off and SAP is updated correctly. Stock visibility matters because SCC needs reservation ownership, not just quantity. Finance visibility matters because customer exposure, reconciliation activity, and daily reporting all depend on seeing across fragmented information. Voice-note and meeting-capture support matter because adoption will be stronger if MAIA fits how people already communicate rather than forcing a rigid reporting behavior that users will resist.

The refined playbook also makes one design implication especially clear: MAIA should match SCC's current operating DNA rather than assume ideal SOP discipline on day one. SCC appears to be a business that keeps moving through relationships, follow-up, and human judgment. MAIA should strengthen that operating style with structure, traceability, and shared visibility. It should not assume the company already works like a perfectly controlled back-office process.

A grounded design priority list for SCC would therefore be:

- pricing request, response, and traceability
- approval flow with clear role permissions before SAP push
- stock reservation visibility with ownership context
- finance visibility across overdue, SOA, and knock-off activity
- lightweight capture of field updates and sales reporting
- a clear answer for how historical records outside SAP will be handled

This narrative also suggests what is lower priority for now. The technical department and work order flow were explicitly noted as not covered at this stage, so the narrative should remain centered on sales, pricing, stock, finance, reporting, and management visibility.
