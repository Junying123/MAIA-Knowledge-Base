# A Day in the Life: Industrial Supplies Distributor

## Before and After MAIA

---

# BEFORE MAIA

## Meet Ah Hock

Ah Hock is 51. He runs Hock Seng Hardware & Industrial Supply Sdn Bhd out of a double-storey shoplot in Sungai Buloh. RM14 million a year in revenue. He supplies fasteners, mild steel bars, welding rods, cutting discs, hand tools, and PPE to about 180 active customers — contractors, factory maintenance teams, hardware shops, and a handful of big developers who buy in bulk.

He has 6 salespeople on the road, 3 admin staff in the office (two handle order processing and invoicing, one handles accounts and collections), and 4 warehouse guys. His wife Susan handles HR and payroll. His son Wei Jie, 26, just came back from his accounting degree and is "learning the business," which mostly means sitting in the office feeling overwhelmed by how chaotic everything is.

Ah Hock built this business from a single hardware shop in 1998. He knows every product by touch. He can tell you the difference between a Grade 8.8 and Grade 10.9 bolt by looking at it. He can quote mild steel bar prices from memory, adjusted for the latest price circular that came in last week. He is the business. And that's the problem.

---

### 6:15 AM — The day starts before the sun is fully up.

Ah Hock is in his car, driving from his house in Damansara to the warehouse. He's already on WhatsApp. Three messages came in overnight.

The first is from Raju, his most senior salesman. Raju forwarded a voice note from a contractor customer — a Hokkien uncle who runs a renovation company in Kepong. The voice note is 47 seconds long. The uncle is rattling off items in a mix of Hokkien and Mandarin, with a TV playing in the background. "Eh, hor wa order ah... simen 50kg lima puluh beg... besi Y12 satu bundle... screw punya, M8 kali 50 itu satu kotak, eh bukan, dua kotak... apa lagi ah... cutting disc 4 inch, itu yang biasa punya, sepuluh keping... hor, itu saja lah, esok pagi hantar ah."

Ah Hock listens to it twice. He thinks he caught everything. He'll have to verify the screw specification later — M8x50 or M8x25? The uncle didn't say the length clearly. And "itu yang biasa punya" for cutting discs — that's the Bosch one they usually buy, but last month the uncle complained about quality and they switched him to a different brand. Which brand is it now? Ah Hock can't remember. He makes a mental note to check.

The second message is from his salesman Danny. Danny typed out an order from a factory maintenance manager — but it's a mess. "Boss, customer want: 1) allen key set metric 2) wd40 big can x 6 3) bearing 6205 — check if got stock 4) cable tie 300mm white 5 pack 5) he also ask about stainless steel rod 304 dia 10mm but I not sure we carry this. Can check?" No quantities for some items. No customer name in the message. Danny does this every time and it drives Ah Hock insane.

The third message came in at 2:47 AM from a contractor who's on a night shift at a construction site in Cyberjaya. It's a photo of a handwritten list on the back of a cement bag — barely legible, smudged, with items half in Malay and half in some shorthand that only makes sense if you know construction supplies. "Paku 3" 10kg, wire mesh BRC A7 — 20 kpg, binding wire 10kg, rebar Y16 x 30 btg 12m, spacer cover 50mm."

Ah Hock squints at the photo while stopped at a traffic light. He can read most of it. The "BRC A7" is probably the standard welded mesh panel they stock. But "20 kpg" — twenty pieces or twenty square metres? He's not sure. He'll need someone to call the contractor back to clarify, but the guy was on night shift and is probably sleeping now.

He puts his phone down. It's not even 6:30 AM and he already has three orders in his head that need to be processed, each one requiring interpretation, clarification, cross-referencing, and manual entry.

He feels tired. Not physically. Just... tired of this being the first thing every single morning.

---

### 7:00 AM — The office opens.

Ah Hock walks in. Mei Ling and Siew Cheng, his two order-processing admin staff, are already at their desks. AutoCount is open on both screens. The WhatsApp Web browser is open next to it.

The morning ritual begins.

Raju, Danny, and the other salespeople start forwarding orders in. Some come as text. Some as photos. Some as voice notes. One salesman, Ah Keat, still calls Mei Ling directly on the phone and reads out orders verbally while she types. Ah Keat is 58 years old and has been with Ah Hock since 2003. He is an incredible salesman — his customers love him, he never loses an account — but he will not type orders into any system, ever. "I sell, you process" is his philosophy, and honestly, Ah Hock can't argue because Ah Keat brings in RM2.5 million a year.

Between 7:00 and 10:00 AM, Mei Ling and Siew Cheng process roughly 25-35 orders. Each order takes 8-15 minutes depending on complexity. Here's what they actually do for each one:

1. Read the WhatsApp message (or listen to the voice note, sometimes three or four times).
    
2. Identify the customer. Sometimes the salesperson says who it's from. Sometimes they don't. Sometimes the message is forwarded from the customer directly, and the customer's WhatsApp name is "David" — but which David? There are four Davids in the system.
    
3. Open the customer's account in AutoCount to check their credit status, payment terms, and last order history.
    
4. For each line item: figure out what the customer actually wants. "Simen 50kg" = Orang Kuat OPC 50kg cement. "Besi Y12" = mild steel round bar Y12 12m. "Screw M8" = but what length? What head type? What material? Call the salesperson back to clarify. Wait. The salesperson is driving. He doesn't answer. Try again in 20 minutes.
    
5. Look up the product in AutoCount. The catalogue has 2,800 SKUs. The search function is basic — you need to know the exact code or a close approximation. "Allen key set metric" could be five different products from three different brands at three different price points. Which one does this customer usually buy?
    
6. Check stock availability. Is it in the Sungai Buloh warehouse? Or the overflow stock in the Rawang store? If it's out of stock, what's the ETA for the next delivery from the supplier?
    
7. Look up the price. Ah, but this customer is on Tier 2 pricing, not Tier 1. And the salesperson gave a verbal discount last time — is that still valid? Check with Ah Hock. Ah Hock is on the phone. Leave it, come back to it later.
    
8. Key in the Sales Order in AutoCount. Double-check quantities. Double-check UOMs — did the customer mean boxes or pieces? "Satu kotak" for a fastener could be 50 pieces, 100 pieces, or 500 pieces depending on the SKU.
    
9. Print the SO. Pass it to the warehouse for picking.
    

Repeat 25-35 times.

By 10:30 AM, Mei Ling has a headache. Not a metaphorical one — a real, throbbing headache behind her left eye. She gets it almost every day. She's been doing this for 7 years. She's thought about quitting, but Ah Hock pays above market and she likes the team. She just hates the mornings.

---

### 10:45 AM — The first error surfaces.

The warehouse calls up. "Eh, this SO for Syarikat Bina Jaya — you wrote Y12 but the contractor specifically said Y16 on the voice note. I remember because he repeated it." Siew Cheng pulls up the original voice note, listens again. Shit. He did say Y16. She heard Y12 because the TV in the background garbled it. She corrects the SO, reprints it, and sends it back down.

This happens on average 2-3 times a day. Most errors are caught at the warehouse stage. Some aren't. The ones that aren't caught become deliveries of the wrong item, which become customer complaints, which become returns, which become credit notes, which become RM500-2,000 in wasted delivery cost and lost product margin per incident.

Ah Hock tracks this loosely. He estimates order errors cost him RM8,000-15,000 a month in direct costs (wrong deliveries, returns, re-delivery) and an unknowable amount in customer trust erosion. Two customers left last year citing "too many mistakes." One of them was a RM30,000/month account.

He doesn't talk about this at dinner. But it sits in his chest like a weight.

---

### 11:30 AM — The pricing call.

Danny calls Ah Hock. "Boss, Syarikat Pembinaan Maju wants 500 pieces of M12x40 hex bolt Grade 8.8. They want a discount — can we do RM0.85 each? Market price is RM0.95."

Ah Hock pulls up the cost in his head. Purchase price is RM0.62. At RM0.85, that's 37% margin. Not great for this volume. But this is a new customer Danny has been chasing for 3 months. If he says no, Danny might lose the deal. If he says yes at RM0.85, it sets a precedent for future orders.

"Give them RM0.88. Tell them it's already special price."

Danny says okay and hangs up. Ah Hock has no way of knowing if Danny will actually quote RM0.88 or if he'll round it down to RM0.85 to close the deal faster. He has no system that enforces the price floor. He trusts his salespeople, mostly — but he's found discrepancies before. Last quarter he discovered that one salesman had been giving a customer 12% off across all categories for six months without approval. That was RM22,000 in leaked margin over 6 months before anyone noticed.

The feeling isn't anger. It's exhaustion. Ah Hock doesn't want to be the pricing police. He wants a system that enforces it so he doesn't have to. But AutoCount is an accounting system, not a pricing enforcement system. And WhatsApp is just... WhatsApp.

---

### 1:00 PM — Lunch. Sort of.

Ah Hock eats at his desk. Nasi campur from the shop downstairs. His phone hasn't stopped vibrating. While he eats, he's:

- Approving a credit limit extension for a customer who wants to buy RM15,000 of welding rods but is already at their RM10,000 limit.
    
- Texting a supplier in China about a delayed shipment of stainless steel plates that was supposed to arrive last week.
    
- Checking WhatsApp messages from two salespeople asking about stock availability for items they could look up themselves if they knew how to use AutoCount, but they don't, so they ask Mei Ling, and when Mei Ling is busy they ask Ah Hock.
    
- Forwarding a payment reminder to a customer who's 67 days overdue on a RM12,000 invoice. This is the third reminder. The customer hasn't responded to the previous two. Ah Hock is considering whether to block their next order or whether that'll push them to a competitor.
    

Wei Jie, his son, is sitting across the office looking at a spreadsheet he built trying to track monthly sales by salesman. The numbers don't reconcile with AutoCount because some orders are still being processed. He asks his father a question about the data and Ah Hock snaps at him — not because the question is stupid, but because Ah Hock is fielding four things at once and doesn't have the bandwidth for a fifth.

Wei Jie goes quiet. He thinks his father doesn't trust him to learn the business. Ah Hock thinks his son doesn't understand how hard this is. Neither of them is wrong.

---

### 3:30 PM — The substitution problem.

A large construction project in Shah Alam needs 304 stainless steel round bar, 10mm diameter. Stock check: zero. Next shipment from supplier: 3 weeks.

If Ah Hock were handling this, he'd know instantly: offer 316 stainless at a small premium — same application, better corrosion resistance, customer probably won't even care about the upgrade. He's done this a hundred times.

But Ah Hock isn't handling this. Siew Cheng is. Siew Cheng doesn't have 25 years of product knowledge. She sees "out of stock," tells the salesperson "sorry, no stock," and the salesperson tells the customer "sorry, no stock." The customer calls another supplier and orders from them.

That's RM3,200 in lost revenue because the substitution knowledge lives only in Ah Hock's head, and Ah Hock was on the phone with the China supplier at 3:30.

This happens multiple times a week. Not always with stainless steel — sometimes it's a specific brand of cutting disc, or a particular grade of bolt. The pattern is the same: out-of-stock means lost sale, because no one else in the company carries the tribal knowledge of what substitutes for what.

---

### 5:15 PM — End of day. Not really.

The warehouse team has loaded 18 deliveries for tomorrow morning. Three SOs are still pending because the salespeople haven't responded to clarification requests. Those orders will carry over to tomorrow morning and add to the pile.

Mei Ling has processed 31 orders today. She made two errors that were caught and corrected. Statistically, there's probably one more that wasn't caught, sitting in a delivery note that'll go out tomorrow and come back as a complaint by Thursday.

Ah Hock does a mental tally of his day:

- 4 pricing decisions he had to make personally
    
- 6 stock inquiries he fielded from salespeople
    
- 2 credit limit issues he had to approve
    
- 1 supplier chase
    
- 1 overdue payment follow-up
    
- 3 order clarifications he helped with because he's the only one who can decode certain customers' shorthand
    
- 0 time spent on business development, strategic planning, or the two new product lines he's been wanting to explore for six months
    

He drives home. In the car, he gets two more WhatsApp messages — a late-night order from a customer who works second shift at a factory, and a voice note from Raju about a potential new account. He'll deal with both in the morning.

At dinner, Susan asks how business is. "Okay lah," he says. He doesn't mention the pricing leak he found this morning, or the lost stainless steel order, or the Y12/Y16 mixup, or the fact that he spent his entire day as a human router instead of running his business.

He feels like a hamster on a wheel. The business is making money — good money, even — but it's entirely dependent on him being the brain at the center of everything. If he gets sick for a week, things fall apart. He knows because it happened in 2022 when he had Covid for 10 days. When he came back, there were RM40,000 in unresolved issues waiting for him.

He's 51. He doesn't want to do this until he's 65. But he doesn't see how to stop, because replacing him means hiring someone who knows 2,800 SKUs, 180 customer relationships, the pricing logic, the substitution logic, and the daily judgment calls — and that person doesn't exist.

Wei Jie could become that person. In 10 years, maybe. Ah Hock doesn't know if the business survives 10 years of his son learning by osmosis.

He falls asleep at 11 PM with WhatsApp still open on his phone, the screen dimming on an unread message.

# AFTER MAIA

## Same Ah Hock. Three months later.

It's been 12 weeks since Hock Seng Hardware onboarded MAIA. The first two weeks were rough — uploading the product catalogue, mapping the customer list, configuring pricing tiers, teaching the system the shorthand that his customers use. Jermaine's team spent a full day at the warehouse understanding the workflow. Wei Jie, surprisingly, took charge of the onboarding. He understood the setup process better than his father did.

Now it's Wednesday.

---

### 6:15 AM — The same drive.

Ah Hock is in his car. His phone vibrates. Same three types of messages as before — a voice note from the Hokkien uncle, a typed order from Danny's customer, a photo of a handwritten list from the construction site contractor.

But this time, Ah Hock doesn't open any of them.

Because none of them were sent to him.

All three were sent by the salespeople to MAIA's WhatsApp number. The salespeople don't message Ah Hock for orders anymore. They forward everything to MAIA — photos, voice notes, text, whatever format the customer sends. Same behaviour, different destination.

Ah Hock opens the MAIA dashboard on his phone. He sees three new orders in the queue, all timestamped within the last 20 minutes. He taps the first one — the voice note from the Hokkien uncle.

MAIA has transcribed it. "Simen 50kg x 50 beg, besi Y12 x 1 bundle (12m), skru M8x50 x 2 kotak, cutting disc 4 inch (Yongkang brand) x 10 keping."

The cutting disc brand is right — "itu yang biasa punya" was matched to the customer's last three orders, which all had Yongkang 4-inch cutting discs. MAIA learned this from order history. The screw specification — M8x50 — was parsed correctly from the Hokkien dialect audio. The quantities are confirmed. The customer was identified by phone number.

Confidence score: 94%. All items matched. Pricing applied automatically — this customer is Tier 2 with standard terms. No exceptions flagged.

The SO draft is already sitting in AutoCount. Nobody had to type anything.

Ah Hock looks at it for five seconds. It's correct. He doesn't touch it.

---

### 7:00 AM — The office opens. But the morning is different.

Mei Ling and Siew Cheng are at their desks. AutoCount is open. But the frantic three-hour processing marathon doesn't happen anymore.

Instead, Mei Ling opens the MAIA exception inbox. There are 4 items flagged out of 22 orders that came in between 6 AM and 7 AM.

Exception 1: Danny's order — the factory maintenance one. MAIA parsed everything except one line: "bearing 6205." MAIA found three matching SKUs (different brands, different price points) and couldn't determine which one the customer wants. Confidence: 67%. Flagged for human review. Mei Ling taps the customer's order history — they bought the NSK 6205 bearing twice before. She selects it. One tap. Done.

Exception 2: The handwritten photo from the construction site. MAIA parsed it correctly except "20 kpg" for the BRC wire mesh. MAIA flagged it: "Ambiguous quantity. '20 kpg' could be 20 pieces or 20 square metres. Customer's last order was 15 pieces. Suggest confirming with customer." Mei Ling sends a pre-written clarification message through MAIA to the salesperson's WhatsApp: "Please confirm with customer — BRC A7: 20 pieces or 20 sqm?" She'll resolve it when the reply comes in.

Exception 3: A new customer order where MAIA doesn't have pricing configured yet. Flagged: "No pricing tier assigned for Syarikat ABC Sdn Bhd. Default to Tier 3?" Mei Ling checks with Danny — yes, new customer, Tier 3 is fine for now. She confirms in one tap. MAIA applies Tier 3 pricing and generates the SO.

Exception 4: A stock issue. Customer ordered 304 stainless steel round bar 10mm. Out of stock. But this time, MAIA doesn't just say "no stock." It triggers the substitution logic: "304 SS round bar 10mm: out of stock. Suggested substitute: 316 SS round bar 10mm, available (23 bars in stock), price premium +8%. Customer has accepted 316 substitute on 2 previous occasions." Mei Ling routes this to the salesperson with the suggestion. The salesperson confirms with the customer in 3 minutes. Sale saved.

Total time for Mei Ling to handle all 4 exceptions: 11 minutes.

The other 18 orders? Already processed. Already in AutoCount. Already in the warehouse pick queue. Nobody touched them.

Siew Cheng, who used to process 15-18 orders every morning, now handles overflow exceptions and has taken on a new role: she's become the customer data quality person. She spends her mornings cleaning up customer records, updating contact details, making sure pricing tiers are current. Work that never got done before because there was no time. She doesn't get headaches anymore.

---

### 10:45 AM — No call from the warehouse.

The warehouse doesn't call about a Y12/Y16 mixup. Because there isn't one. MAIA parsed the voice note correctly, the confidence gating caught the audio quality issue in a different order and routed it for review, and the orders that went straight through were accurate.

Ah Hock doesn't know that nothing happened. That's the point. The absence of problems is invisible. But Wei Jie, who has access to the MAIA dashboard, is tracking it. Week 11: zero order errors. Week 10: one error (a UOM confusion that was caught at the exception stage). Week 9: zero.

Before MAIA, they were averaging 8-12 errors per week. Some caught before delivery, some not.

Wei Jie made a chart. He hasn't shown his father yet because he's not sure Ah Hock cares about charts. But the numbers are stark: order processing errors down 96%. Customer returns due to wrong items: from 11 per month to 1 per month. Estimated monthly savings from avoided returns, re-deliveries, and credit notes: RM11,400.

---

### 11:30 AM — The pricing call that doesn't happen.

Danny has a new customer who wants 500 pieces of M12x40 hex bolt Grade 8.8 at RM0.85 each. Before, Danny would have called Ah Hock to ask.

Now Danny keys the order into MAIA with the requested price. MAIA checks: purchase price RM0.62, floor price for Tier 3 (new customer) is RM0.90, Danny's requested price of RM0.85 is below floor.

MAIA blocks the SO and sends Danny a WhatsApp: "Price RM0.85 for M12x40 hex bolt 8.8 is below floor price (RM0.90) for Tier 3 customers. Options: (1) Apply floor price RM0.90, (2) Request manager approval for special price."

Danny taps "Request manager approval." Ah Hock gets a WhatsApp notification from MAIA: "Price approval request from Danny. Customer: Syarikat Pembinaan Maju. Item: M12x40 hex bolt 8.8 x 500 pcs. Requested: RM0.85/pc. Floor: RM0.90/pc. Cost: RM0.62/pc. Margin at requested price: 37.1%. Approve / Reject / Counter-offer."

Ah Hock sees it while he's having coffee. He taps "Counter-offer" and types "0.88". MAIA sends it back to Danny: "Manager approved at RM0.88/pc. SO updated."

Total elapsed time: 4 minutes. No phone call. No ambiguity. No possibility of Danny going rogue at RM0.85. The pricing decision is logged with Ah Hock's approval timestamp.

Over the last 3 months, MAIA's pricing enforcement has caught 34 below-floor quotes. Ah Hock approved 12 of them with counter-offers, rejected 6, and 16 were corrected to floor price by the salespeople themselves after seeing the notification. Estimated margin leakage prevented: RM7,200/month.

Ah Hock doesn't have to be the pricing police anymore. The system is the police. He's the judge — and only when it matters.

---

### 1:00 PM — Lunch. Actually lunch.

Ah Hock eats nasi campur at the shop downstairs. Not at his desk. He sits with two of his salespeople who are back from morning rounds. They talk about a new housing development in Rawang that's going to need a lot of rebar and fittings — a potential RM200K project. Ah Hock hasn't thought about business development like this in years. He's usually too buried in operations to chase new opportunities.

His phone buzzes once: MAIA's daily midday summary. "22 orders processed. 4 exceptions resolved. 1 pending (awaiting customer clarification on BRC quantity). 0 credit holds. Total order value: RM47,300."

He glances at it for three seconds and puts his phone away.

---

### 3:30 PM — The substitution that sells itself.

Same situation as the "before" story — customer needs 304 stainless steel round bar, 10mm. Out of stock. But Ah Hock isn't involved at all.

MAIA has already flagged the substitution suggestion to the salesperson: "316 SS round bar 10mm available. +8% price premium. Customer has accepted 316 substitute previously." The salesperson calls the customer, offers the 316 at a small premium, customer agrees. Order goes through.

Ah Hock finds out about it at the end of the day when he glances at the dashboard. RM3,200 in revenue that would have walked out the door. He didn't have to lift a finger.

He thinks about all the times this happened before — all the "sorry, no stock" responses that could have been "we have an alternative" — and feels a mix of relief and mild regret that he didn't have this system five years ago.

---

### 4:00 PM — Wei Jie has a conversation with his father.

This is the part nobody expected.

Wei Jie has been running the MAIA dashboard for 3 months. He understands the data now — which customers are growing, which are shrinking, which salespeople are closing well, which products have the highest margin, where the stock shortages keep hitting. He's built a simple analysis showing that 40% of their revenue comes from 22 customers, and 6 of those customers haven't increased their order volume in 18 months.

He walks over to his father's desk and says, "Pa, I think we should do a pricing review for our top accounts. Some of them haven't had a price adjustment in two years. And there are three accounts that are ordering more but we haven't moved them to Tier 1 yet — we could lock them in with better pricing before they start looking elsewhere."

Ah Hock looks at him. This is the first time Wei Jie has come to him with a business insight instead of a data question. Not "the numbers don't match" — but "here's what the numbers mean and what we should do about it."

This happened because Wei Jie is no longer spending his days reconciling messy spreadsheets with incomplete AutoCount data. MAIA's reporting gives him clean, real-time numbers. He has the bandwidth to think, not just count.

Ah Hock doesn't say "good job" — that's not his style. He says, "Okay, show me the numbers after dinner tonight." But inside, something shifts. For the first time, he can see how his son might actually run this business.

That's worth more than any efficiency metric.

---

### 5:15 PM — End of day.

Ah Hock does his mental tally:

- 0 pricing decisions he had to make (the one approval took 30 seconds)
    
- 0 stock inquiries he fielded from salespeople (they ask MAIA now)
    
- 0 order clarifications he helped with (MAIA or the exception inbox handled them)
    
- 1 credit limit extension he approved (via MAIA notification, one tap)
    
- 1 supplier call he made (China shipment — this one MAIA can't help with yet)
    
- 1 business development conversation about the Rawang housing project
    
- 1 strategic discussion with his son about pricing and account management
    

He drives home. His phone is quieter. Not silent — he still gets messages — but the operational noise is filtered. MAIA handles the routine. Exceptions route to the right people. He only sees what actually needs his judgment.

At dinner, Susan asks how business is. "Good," he says. Then he adds, "Wei Jie showed me something interesting about our top accounts today. I think the boy is getting it."

Susan smiles. She's been waiting to hear that for two years.

---

### Friday morning — The weekly report.

Ah Hock's phone buzzes at 8:00 AM. MAIA's weekly summary, delivered via WhatsApp:

Hock Seng Hardware — Week 12 Summary

- Orders processed: 147
    
- Auto-processed (no human touch): 119 (81%)
    
- Exceptions resolved: 28
    
- Unresolved exceptions: 0
    
- Order errors reaching customer: 0
    
- Estimated time saved: 54 hours (vs. manual baseline)
    
- Below-floor pricing attempts caught: 8
    
- Substitution suggestions accepted: 3 (RM8,700 in revenue saved)
    
- Collection reminders sent: 12
    
- Payments received after reminder: 7 (RM31,200 collected)
    

Ah Hock reads it in 30 seconds. He doesn't analyse every number. But he sees the trend — 81% of orders are fully automated now. That means Mei Ling and Siew Cheng are handling only the genuinely tricky 19%, and they're doing it better and faster because they're not exhausted from the other 81%.

He forwards the report to Wei Jie with one word: "Good."

---

### What changed, really.

It's not that MAIA replaced Mei Ling or Siew Cheng. They're both still there. Mei Ling is now spending half her day on supplier coordination — something Ah Hock used to do himself. Siew Cheng is managing customer data and helping with the quarterly pricing review that Wei Jie proposed. The warehouse team is picking more accurately because the SOs are more accurate. The salespeople are spending less time on admin and more time visiting customers — Danny closed three new accounts last month, the most he's ever done.

The thing Ah Hock can't put a number on, but feels in his bones: he's not the bottleneck anymore.

He still knows every product. He still makes the big calls. He's still the boss. But the business doesn't collapse when he takes a day off. When he went for a medical checkup last Tuesday and was out until 2 PM, he came back to zero fires. MAIA processed 23 orders, Mei Ling handled 5 exceptions, and Wei Jie approved one pricing request using the authority Ah Hock had delegated to him for anything under RM500.

For the first time in 26 years of running this business, Ah Hock feels like he owns the business instead of the business owning him.

He doesn't say this out loud. He's not that kind of person. But last weekend, he told Susan they should plan a holiday to Taiwan in June. They haven't taken a real holiday since 2019.

That says everything.
